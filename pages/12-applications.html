12 — Applications

Color science shows up everywhere: how your eyes adapt to lighting, how cameras process images, how printers make reliable proofs, and how we design for accessibility.

Big idea: “The color” is not just a property of an object. It depends on illumination, materials, devices, and human vision.

Four places you meet color science

  • Eyes: your brain adjusts to different light.
  • Cameras: they guess white balance.
  • Printing: proofs help predict final color on paper.
  • Design: not everyone sees color the same way.

Practical wins

  • Make photos look consistent across lighting (white balance, adaptation).
  • Match brand colors across screens and printers (profiles, ΔE tolerances).
  • Design UIs that remain readable for more people (contrast + redundancy).

When “ground truth” is tricky

There’s no single absolute “correct” color without specifying viewing conditions. In printing: D50 viewing, standard observers, and profiling define a workable reference. In vision: adaptation and context mean perception can shift even for identical pixels.

Color constancy (vision)

Your visual system partly “discounts” the illuminant. That’s why a white shirt looks white in sunlight and also under warm indoor lighting — even though the pixels (spectra) differ.

🧠
Key idea: perception depends on context. Surrounding colors affect what you see.
Move toward warm (yellow) or cool (blue) “lighting.”
0% = no constancy. 100% = strong compensation.

Camera white balance (WB)

Cameras estimate the illuminant and apply gains to R/G/B so neutrals look neutral. If WB is wrong, whites look tinted.

Pipeline idea sensor → WB → tone/gamut → display

Print proofing & tolerances

Proofing tries to predict how colors will look on a specific press/paper. If a color is out-of-gamut, it must be mapped. QA often uses ΔE thresholds.

📏
QA idea: “Is this close enough?” becomes a number (ΔE) plus a spec.
Left = preserve saturation (clip-ish). Right = preserve relationships (compress-ish).
Lower = smaller printer gamut.

Accessibility (color vision differences)

Some people have reduced sensitivity to certain cone types (e.g., red-green confusion). Good design avoids relying on color alone: add labels, shapes, and ensure contrast.

What you learned

Short recap.

  • Perception adapts to lighting (constancy).
  • Cameras estimate white balance to keep neutrals neutral.
  • Printing uses profiles, mapping, and ΔE-based tolerances.
  • Accessible design uses contrast and redundancy, not color alone.
