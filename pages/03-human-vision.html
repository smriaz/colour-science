03 — Human vision

Your eye has three kinds of “color sensors” called cones. They don’t measure wavelength directly — they respond in wide, overlapping bands.

Big idea: Many different spectra can trigger the same cone responses. That’s why color matching is possible — and why printing and lighting can create surprises.

Three cone teams

  • S cones: like “bluish” helpers
  • M cones: like “greenish” helpers
  • L cones: like “reddish” helpers

Your brain compares these teams to decide what color you see.

Cones + comparisons

Cones are often named by their peak sensitivity: S (short), M (medium), L (long). What you perceive depends heavily on relative signals, not absolute values.

  • Then the brain forms opponent channels (roughly: red–green and blue–yellow).
  • This helps explain afterimages and why some colors feel “opposite.”

Color matching and metamerism

The retina compresses a full spectrum (hundreds of wavelength samples) down to ~3 cone responses (L, M, S). Many SPDs map to the same LMS triplet — those are metamers.

This is foundational for tristimulus colorimetry (e.g., CIE XYZ) and explains printing/lighting mismatches.

What you learned

Short recap.

  • Humans typically have three cone types (S/M/L) with overlapping sensitivity curves.
  • Color is built from comparisons of cone signals (opponent processing).
  • Reducing spectra to 3 numbers explains metamerism and why matching is possible.
