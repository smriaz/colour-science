04 — RGB & displays

Screens create color by adding light from three primaries: Red, Green, Blue. This is called additive mixing.

Big idea: RGB works because matching the cone responses in your eyes is enough to match perceived color for most people.

Adding light = brighter

  • Red light + Green light → looks yellow
  • Green + Blue → looks cyan
  • Red + Blue → looks magenta
  • All three high → white

This is different from mixing paints (we’ll do printing later!).

Why three primaries?

Your eye has three cone types (S/M/L). A display can adjust its three primaries so the resulting light triggers similar cone responses to the real thing.

  • Not because “there are only three colors in the world.”
  • Because three controls can match three cone signals (approximately).

Tristimulus matching

For a given observer and viewing conditions, many spectra reduce to a 3D response space (e.g., LMS or CIE XYZ). Displays exploit this by emitting three fixed spectra and varying their intensities.

Caveat: metameric matches can break under different observers or lighting; gamut limits also matter.

What you learned

Short recap.

  • Screens mix color by adding light from R, G, and B.
  • RGB works because it can match cone responses (tristimulus matching).
  • RGB “distance” is not the same as perceived difference — we’ll improve that later.
